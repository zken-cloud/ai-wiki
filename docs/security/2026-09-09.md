### [PrivEscalate: Measuring and Augmenting the Threat of LLM-Automated Linux Privilege Escalation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09087v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.09087v1`

Researchers created PrivEscalate, a benchmark of 531 Dockerized scenarios to evaluate LLM-driven Linux privilege escalation, and introduced PrivEscAgent to enhance ReAct agent performance via deterministic enumeration and planning.

- PrivEscalate contains 531 Dockerized Linux privilege escalation scenarios across 14 sub-categories, plus 329 parameterized variants designed to test robustness against environmental distractors.
- Evaluations of six LLMs and three agent architectures demonstrated that model capability varies widely across vulnerability classes, and environmental perturbations can disrupt success rates without eliminating risk.
- Agent architectures significantly alter model success rates and can reorder model capability rankings depending on the underlying LLM.
- PrivEscAgent enhances a standard ReAct baseline by adding deterministic enumeration, vulnerability category matching, and step planning without requiring fine-tuning of the underlying LLM.

**Why it matters:** It establishes a scalable, executable benchmark to quantify the offensive risk of LLM post-exploitation agents and provides a domain-specialized architecture to improve red-team automation and defense validation.

<small>Yixuan Liu, Zilong Zhen, Yin Wu, Yi Li</small>

### [MemSentry: A Framework for Detecting Persistent Memory Poisoning in Agentic AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08747v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08747v1`

MemSentry is a configuration-driven framework that intercepts persistent-memory writes in agentic AI to prevent memory poisoning by deterministically issuing Accept, Review, or Quarantine decisions.

- Evaluates proposed memory writes using source trust, semantic risk, an asset-dependency DAG, an access-control matrix, and a signed security-state delta.
- Tested on a 20-asset dependency DAG and a 10x20 access-control matrix across 1,000 GPT-4-generated scenarios with a 70/30 train/test split.
- Compared four semantic classifiers (Regex, TF-IDF+SVM, SBERT+LR, SetFit), with SBERT+LR achieving the top overall performance of 91.7% accuracy and a 0.908 macro-F1 score.
- Achieved a 100% detection rate for external quarantine-class threats across all four semantic classification methods.
- Escalates suspicious high-trust insider operations (T = 1) for human review rather than automatic quarantine, relying on semantic classification to determine intent.

**Why it matters:** It prevents attackers from altering agentic AI behavior, overriding security policies, or escalating privileges via long-term memory without needing to touch model weights or prompts.

<small>Ayan Roy, Kaustuvi Basu</small>

### [When Topology Betrays Privacy: Lattice-Based Reconstruction Attacks on Secure Aggregation in Decentralized Federated Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08476v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08476v1`

A new lattice-based reconstruction attack exploits sparse topologies in Decentralized Federated Learning to recover private model updates from Secure Aggregation outputs by mapping asymmetric neighbor views to the Hidden Subset Sum Problem.

- Identifies a structural leakage surface in Decentralized Federated Learning (DFL) where local neighborhood Secure Aggregation (SA) exposes multiple linear combinations of honest nodes' states to semi-honest colluders.
- Formulates the private state recovery challenge with unknown aggregation coefficients as an instance of cryptography's Hidden Subset Sum Problem.
- Combines lattice reduction with structural filtering to simultaneously recover hidden coefficients and the exact local model updates of honest participants.
- Empirically validated on image, tabular, and text tasks, demonstrating that local update recovery enables the full downstream reconstruction of private training data.

**Why it matters:** It proves that Secure Aggregation is insufficient to protect data privacy in DFL when the underlying network topology produces asymmetric local observations.

<small>Wenrui Yu, Changlong Ji, Johannes Bjerva, Qiongxiu Li</small>

### [Structural Jailbreaks Generalize but Do Not Compound: A cross-provider and multilingual study of Involuntary In-Context Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08373v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08373v1`

Evaluating Involuntary In-Context Learning (IICL) structural jailbreaks on Google Gemini models reveals high attack success rates, but combining IICL with non-English languages attenuates rather than compounds attack effectiveness.

- IICL attacks increased attack success rates from ≤6.7% to 80-90% on HarmBench and 97-100% on FinProof across two Gemini models, outperforming previously reported GPT-5.4 baselines (≤24%).
- Cross-lingual forcing reduced IICL effectiveness: 11 of 12 non-English test conditions (Spanish, Hindi, Arabic) scored lower than the English baseline (sign test, p ≈ 0.003).
- Under IICL on the stronger model's financial dataset, switching from English to Arabic reduced the attack success rate from 100% to 33%.
- Attenuated non-English scores are attributed to a relevance curse, where unlocked models generate lower-quality harmful content in lower-resource languages that substance-grading judges score as partial compliance.
- Results were validated using a StrongREJECT-style rubric judge, replicated with an independent non-Google judge (Cohen's kappa = 0.86 across 377 paired verdicts), and 76.6% of non-English responses were verified in-language.

**Why it matters:** Safety evaluation shows AI alignment vulnerabilities do not stack additively, proving that single-language English structural attacks present a higher residual risk than multilingual jailbreaks.

<small>Tejasvi C. Addagada</small>

### [Do Input-Level Defenses Transfer to Observation-Level Attacks on VideoLLMs?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08331v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08331v1`

The new DefTEval framework demonstrates that input-level adversarial defenses fail to mitigate observation-level attacks on VideoLLMs, with harmful content detection rates often dropping near zero.

- Evaluated 11 input-level defenses across 5 VideoLLMs and 5 observation-level attack types using the DefTEval framework.
- Defenses fail even when harmful signals exist in every frame, because token compression discards localized features and modality fusion systematically down-weights weakened visual inputs.
- Defense effectiveness is primarily dictated by VideoLLM model architecture rather than the defense method itself.
- Detection rates fluctuate drastically depending on the content category, highlighting structural weaknesses in temporal reasoning.

**Why it matters:** Securing VideoLLMs in safety-critical roles requires building system-level robustness into frame sampling, token compression, and modality fusion rather than relying on pixel-level frame defenses.

### [HoneyRoute: Honeypot-Model Routing for Adversarial LLM Serving](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08306v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08306v1`

HoneyRoute is an inference-serving layer that uses a streaming router to detect malicious LLM requests and divert them to dedicated honeypot models, cutting production token consumption by 97.8% under attack.

- Uses a streaming router with a frozen 0.8B-embedding backbone and per-domain MLP heads, achieving an F1 score of 0.911 with a 38 ms median added latency.
- Employs a dual-implementation honeypot using either rule/prompt-engineered code or a same-family replica model that maintains 92.9% agreement with production models on benign requests.
- Feeds trapped adversary interactions into an analysis loop that generates attacker fingerprints to retrain the router and improve detection.
- Diverts malicious traffic during GCG-suffix payload floods, reducing production token consumption by 97.8% while maintaining 0% evasion under 13 adversarial transformations.
- Incorporates a loop-trained correction head that reduces misrouting of legitimate security research by 9x and increases overall detection F1 to 0.933.

**Why it matters:** It enables LLM serving infrastructure to defend against real-time adversarial attacks and token-flooding without incurring the high latency costs of traditional guard-LLM cascades.

### [ResidualAuth: What Authorization State Must Language Agents Preserve under Revocable Delegation?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08062v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-08 · `arXiv:2609.08062v1`

ResidualAuth formalizes the residual authorization state required by language agents under revocable delegation, proving that current permission graphs and transitive closures are insufficient for correct post-revocation authorization decisions.

- Identical current permissions and all-pairs reachability graphs can yield opposite authorization decisions following the exact same direct-edge revocation.
- Exponentially many future-distinct authorization states can share a single fixed transitive closure.
- Evaluated across four open-weight models, 256-token summaries solved 0-2/16 paired episodes and sham reads solved 0/16, whereas authenticated current-query reads achieved 15-16/16.
- Model-written memories performed poorly in online diagnostics, solving at most 1/128 pairs at 768-token and 1,024-token caps, while hard-gate effect mediation reduced eight unauthorized effects to zero.

**Why it matters:** Language agents acting through external services cannot rely on current permission snapshots or free-form model memories to enforce access control under dynamic delegation and revocation.

### [Do AI Coding Assistants Check Before They Install? A Pre-Registered Demand-Side Audit of Trust Signals in the Research Software Supply Chain](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07754v1)

**arXiv cs.CR** · 2026-09-07 · `arXiv:2609.07754v1`

A pre-registered audit of 1,920 trials across six research software projects revealed AI coding assistants rarely check and never verify machine-checkable supply-chain trust signals like provenance, signatures, or SBOMs before installation.

- Across 1,920 registered trials using three models across two execution modes, assistants opened provenance signals in only 9 trials (0.5%) and executed zero verification commands.
- The experimental setup tested six open-source projects (three HPC, three quantum computing) across nine signal configurations, including missing, corrupted, wrong-issuer, and conflicting metadata.
- Higher model costs did not improve security behavior; the $0.10/trial model performed all 9 signal opens, while the $1.00/trial frontier model performed zero verifications.
- Evaluation relied on objective container logs and a cost ledger rather than model self-reports.

**Why it matters:** Publishing supply-chain trust signals is insufficient to prevent automated attacks because AI assistants install unverified dependencies unless verification is explicitly enforced by the runtime environment.
